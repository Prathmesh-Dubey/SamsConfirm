The sites · CNRL

What is classified across Horizon, Albian and the thermal plants

Horizon is a mine, extraction plant and upgrader about 75 kilometres north of Fort McMurray. Albian comprises the Muskeg River and Jackpine mines, whose bitumen goes south to the Scotford Upgrader near Fort Saskatchewan, so the Albian chain spans two very different parts of Alberta. CNRL also operates the thermal side: Jackfish near Conklin uses SAGD, while Primrose, near Cold Lake, uses cyclic steam stimulation.

The distinction matters for a handset. Cyclic steam injects steam into a well, soaks, then produces it back, so a pad is a rotating set of wells at different stages with surface pipework carrying hot, high-pressure fluid and gas. SAGD pairs run continuously and feed central processing facilities. At Horizon and Scotford, classified areas surround froth treatment, upgrading units, hydrogen supply and the coke and sulphur handling that bring dust as well as gas.

What two extraction methods ask of a handset

Most oil sands operators pick one method. CNRL has both, and the working pattern of a mining shift and a pad round are nothing alike.

Mining shifts and pad rounds on one console

A Horizon maintenance crew and a Jackfish pad operator want different things from a phone. Enrolment through the existing EMM and MDM lets CNRL define separate profiles on one handset model, so training and spares stay simple.

Dispersed pads and long drives

Primrose and Jackfish pads sit apart across muskeg and bush roads. Four-constellation GNSS and a 4,000 mAh battery suit rounds measured in hours between charging points.

Dust at the upgrader

Coke and sulphur handling at Horizon and Scotford are dust locations. The EX-04Z1 is designed for Zone 1 gas and Zone 21 dust, so one handset is meant to cover both hazards in the same plant.

Cold on the thermal pads

Alberta winter doesn’t pause for a steam pad. The temperature range printed on the certificate, once issued, is what an electrical inspector will read, and CNRL’s own engineers will check it against pad duty.

How work is organised

Rotations, a long supply chain and turnaround seasons

CNRL moves bitumen between sites hundreds of kilometres apart, and the people who maintain that chain move too.

Rotations
Fort McMurray and Cold Lake area sites rely on rotation staffing and camps. Phones are issued on arrival and returned or wiped on departure, so a clean enrolment process matters.
Turnarounds
Upgrader and plant shutdowns bring large contractor crews through classified areas on a planned schedule. Device control at the gate is part of site access.
Two supply chains
Mining, upgrading and thermal operations each have their own contractors. A common handset makes the fleet simpler to manage but does not remove each site’s own engineering sign-off.
Approval route
We have not verified a CNRL vendor registration programme. Ask CNRL’s procurement and HSE teams what they require, and expect the certificate and area drawings to lead the review.
Questions · Canadian Natural Resources

Frequently asked questions

Is Primrose a SAGD site?

No. Primrose uses cyclic steam stimulation, in which a well is injected with steam, soaked, then produced. Jackfish uses SAGD. Both are thermal in situ, but the pad operating pattern differs, which matters when planning rounds with a handset.

Does CNRL need one device for mining and another for in situ?

The design intent is one handset standard. Each site’s area classification and CNRL’s own engineering review decide where it may be used.

Why does Scotford matter for a CNRL evaluation?

Albian bitumen is upgraded at Scotford, near Fort Saskatchewan, so CNRL equipment questions extend south into the Heartland. Compare it with the explosion proof phone guide for Canadian refining and chemicals.

How does this differ from the Cenovus page?

Both operate in situ plants, but they have separate procurement and engineering. See explosion proof mobile phone for Cenovus Energy; Cenovus does not run oil sands mining.
